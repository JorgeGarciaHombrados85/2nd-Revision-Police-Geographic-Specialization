
%% Comment 2. Policy impacts and increases in police resources
%At present, the paper provides too little detail, leaving the reader unsure about how to interpret results of a reform that changed multiple aspects of policing.

%2.1- The policy seems to have changed for some cities the allocation of resources conditional on total levels, and for some others the aggregate levels too. The authors attempt to address this in Figure 5, however a few points are still unclear. In Panels C and D, the first bar seems to suggest that even the control group experienced increases in the number of officers. Could the authors clarify why this is the case, and how large these increases were relative to baseline levels? In addition, Panel E and F show the effects on crime perceptions and reported crime for municipalities with small versus large increases in police forces. The point estimates differ, and the overlapping confidence intervals may simply reflect low statistical power (given a sample size of N=200/300). Thus, the conclusion that “resource increases alone were not sufficient to improve police effectiveness” seems premature. Perhaps more importantly, this would contrast with much of the existing literature on police strength and crime, and it would be helpful if the authors clarified how they interpret their findings in light of this.

Thanks for the opportunity to clarify this point. Increasingly concerned about growing crime and crime perception, successive Chilean governments substantially increased police resources during the 2000s and 2010s.\footnote{Between 2004 and 2006, the Lagos government incorporated 2,970 new Carabineros. The Bachelet government (2006--2010) added a further 6,000 officers, and the Piñera government (2010--2014) committed to 10,000 additional officers, a target achieved through a combination of new hires and the reallocation of existing officers from administrative to operational duties.} As a result, both treatment and control municipalities experienced substantial increases in resources during the study period, which explains why the control group also shows increases in the number of officers in Figure \ref{fig:figure05}. For example, during this period, the average yearly increase in the number of officers in a never treated or not yet treated municipality was 9.7\%, and that of patrol vehicles was 6\%.

A key premise for the successful territorial reorganization introduced by \emph{Plan Cuadrante} was that municipalities had sufficient resources to staff the new quadrants. With this goal in mind, some adopting municipalities received significant increases in officers and patrol vehicles relative to the control group, though budget constraints meant that the target of closing pre-existing deficits---measured in Equivalent Surveillance Units (UVEs)---was rarely fully achieved, and the size of the deficit does not appear to have been the main criterion determining the increase a municipality received \citep{DIPRES2007_largo, DIPRES2014}.\footnote{We requested access to information on the demand, supply, and deficit of UVEs from Carabineros, but this information was not shared.} The additional officers were predominantly newly hired rather than reallocated from control municipalities, as transfers across Carabineros units are centrally regulated and subject to minimum permanence periods of up to ten years \citep{DIPRES2014}. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:figure_resources}, the resource increase was nonetheless highly uneven: while municipalities in the bottom 40\% of the resource-increase distribution upon joining \emph{Plan Cuadrante} received increases in resources very similar to those observed in never-treated or not-yet-treated municipalities, municipalities in the top 40\% experienced substantially larger increases, with officers rising by around 22\% per year and patrol vehicles by around 80\% per year. Reassuringly, municipalities receiving larger versus smaller resource increases---whether the split is based on the increase in police officers or in patrol vehicles---do not display differential pre-treatment trends in either the outcomes of interest or in police resources themselves, which alleviates concerns that this split might itself be confounded by pre-existing resource or crime trends across municipalities.\footnote{The p-values for the joint test of equal pre-trends between the two groups are, for the officers-based split and the patrol-vehicles-based split respectively: 0.823 and 0.048 for victimization, 0.059 and 0.379 for crime perception, 0.820 and 0.157 for investment in security measures, 0.213 and 0.419 for reported crime, 0.616 and 0.444 for police officers, and 0.405 and 0.157 for patrol vehicles.} 

Regarding panels (c) and (d) of Figure \ref{fig:figure_resources}, we agree that the overlapping confidence intervals may partly reflect limited statistical power, and we have been careful not to overstate our conclusions on this point in the revised manuscript. We do not argue that resources play no role: they likely enable the successful implementation of the program, and for some outcomes such as crime perception the effects may indeed be larger in municipalities with greater resource increases. In the absence of a natural experiment, we cannot estimate the causal effect of resources in isolation in the context of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}.\footnote{We requested information on the supply and demand of UVEs, which might provide a proxy for the planned increase in resources at the time of program adoption, but this information was not shared.} Our argument is different: the beat policing component of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}---the division of the municipal territory into quadrants with each officer in the municipality permanently assigned to one quadrant rather than rotating across the full municipality---plays a key role in explaining the results, over and above the resource channel. This is supported by the fact that municipalities with no resource increases relative to the control group (municipalities in the bottom 40\% of the resource-increase distribution) still display sizeable and statistically significant effects for most outcomes. For some outcomes such as victimization or household investments in prevention measures, the magnitude is very similar to the effect of the program in municipalities that receive large resource increases. While for crime perception the effect is smaller, it is also significant at conventional confidence levels. On the other hand, the effect on reported crime has the expected sign and is sizeable, although statistically insignificant, which might be attributable to lower statistical power in this analysis, since unlike the survey-based outcomes it is conducted at the municipality rather than at the household level. In line with the relevance of the beat policing component, we also show in the updated manuscript that increases in police resources unrelated to \emph{Plan Cuadrante} have limited effects on the same outcomes as discussed below. 


We also want to clarify that we do not view our results as being in tension with the existing literature on police strength and crime. As \citet{chalfin2025investments} notes in his review of the literature, the effectiveness of additional police resources depends crucially on how they are organized and deployed, suggesting that organizational reforms of the kind studied here can have important effects on public safety independently of changes in resource levels.
