We thank the reviewer for this comment. We agree that ``geographic specialization'' may be misleading, and we have changed the terminology used to describe the program. The intervention we study is known in the criminological literature and police practice as beat policing, the permanent assignment of officers to smaller, fixed catchment areas rather than rotating across larger ones. Beat policing has been widely implemented across police forces worldwide and connects directly to Ostrom's seminal hypothesis about why smaller police departments outperform larger ones \citep{ostrom1973}. Despite its relevance, it has not been rigorously evaluated in the economics or criminology literatures. We used ``geographic specialization'', reasoning that it would be more intuitive for an economics audience and could connect better with the literature on local public service delivery, but we recognize its limitations. In the revised manuscript, we use the term ``beat policing'' and connect it to the literature on territorial/local specialization in public service delivery \citep{besley2003centralized}, which we believe makes the concept more intuitive for an economics audience. If the editor or reviewer is not convinced by the use of ``beat policing'' either, we would welcome guidance on preferred terminology. Alternative formulations we have considered include ``territorial reorganization'' and ``local police assignment''.

Regarding the comparison with hot spot policing, \emph{Plan Cuadrante} is not hot spot policing. The latter approach concentrates resources in high-crime areas but does not necessarily involve permanent beat assignments, nor does it focus on building local knowledge and community ties. It is typically implemented through targeted interventions at specific locations rather than systematic territorial organization, and the same officers are not necessarily sent to the same locations repeatedly. Evidence on its effectiveness is mixed, with some studies finding crime reductions in targeted areas but others documenting potential displacement to neighboring zones \citep{SR_1}. \emph{Plan Cuadrante}, by contrast, subdivided entire urban territories into quadrants with \emph{permanent} officer assignment, ensuring stable and knowledgeable coverage across the full territory rather than concentrating resources where crime is highest. As a result, while some quadrants contained crime hot-spots, many did not, and this comprehensive territorial approach potentially mitigates the displacement effects associated with hot-spot policing.

We discuss these differences explicitly in the updated Section 2 of the manuscript:

\begin{adjustwidth}{2em}{0em}
Although beat policing shares features with other policing approaches, such as hot-spot policing and community-based policing, which have been widely studied, it has distinctive features that make it worth studying as an independent approach. Hot-spot policing concentrates resources in high-crime areas and does not focus on building local knowledge and community ties. It is typically implemented through targeted interventions at specific locations rather than systematic territorial organization, and the same officers are not necessarily sent repeatedly to the same areas. Evidence on hot-spot policing is mixed, with some studies showing crime reductions in targeted areas but also potential displacement effects \citep{SR_1}. \emph{Plan Cuadrante}, by contrast, subdivided all urban municipalities into quadrants with permanent officer assignment. As Panel (b) of Figure \ref{fig:figure01} illustrates, some quadrants contained crime hot-spots, but many did not. The reform thus addressed entire urban territories rather than specific high-crime areas, potentially mitigating displacement effects associated with hot-spot policing.
\end{adjustwidth}



Regarding response time reforms, program documentation does not include any explicit objective of reducing response times \citep{DIPRES2007_largo, DIPRES2014}. The reform did not build new police stations; rather, it redefined the territorial responsibilities of officers within already existing police stations \citep{DIPRES2007_largo}. Throughout our study period, a single national set of operational guidelines applied to officers in both treated and control municipalities, with no formal directive to change police tactics to improve response times \citep{Manual_Carabineros2018, DIPRES2007_largo}. Nonetheless, response times could have improved as a byproduct of the increase in patrol vehicles or police officers that accompanied the program in some municipalities. While we lack information on response times to directly test their role, the fact that the program had similarly large effects on victimization in municipalities where resource increases were similar to those in the control group suggests that response times are unlikely to be the main mechanism driving the effects. The mechanism we propose operates through a different channel: by fixing the same officers to the same territory rather than rotating them across a larger territory, the reform generates accumulated local knowledge that increases police effectiveness and deterrence. 


Regarding whether the reform included decentralization of decision-making, the program formally created the role of Quadrant Delegate, a non-commissioned officer with primary operational responsibility for their assigned quadrant, which represented a certain degree of decentralization of day-to-day operational decisions from the station commander to the frontline officer. This autonomy was however constrained by the hierarchical command structure within police stations, which remained unchanged. 

While Plan Cuadrante does not entail any explicit change in police tactics during the period studied \citep{DIPRES2014}, and officers operate under the same operational guidelines without any formal directive to shift personnel from office to street duties, in some municipalities the program was accompanied by an increase in police resources, driven primarily by new hires rather than reallocation, which plausibly increased the number of officers on the street. Consistent with this, we show that Plan Cuadrante increased perceived police presence in neighborhoods. However, we also show that the program had large effects on perceived police presence in municipalities where the increase in the number of officers was very similar to that in the control group, suggesting that the reduction in the catchment area plays an important role in police visibility beyond the resource channel.

Finally, Plan Cuadrante should not be confused with community policing. While the program's original design envisaged a formal community integration component, independent program evaluations document that this component existed only on paper throughout our study period \citep{DIPRES2007_largo, DIPRES2014}. The community integration model (MICC) was piloted in only four police stations in 2012--2013 (in police stations not included in the analytical sample) before its financing agreement was discontinued in early 2014, and its large-scale implementation only materialized with Plan Cuadrante 2.0 after the end of our study period. Any improvement in police-community relations during the period we study therefore did not result from an explicit tactical change or the formal integration of the community into the prevention strategy, but rather from the more continuous and stable contact that naturally arises when the same officers are permanently assigned to the same territory.
