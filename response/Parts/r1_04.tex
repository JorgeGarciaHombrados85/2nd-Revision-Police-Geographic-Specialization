Thanks for the suggestions. Let us start by clarifying our main claim, which we have stated more precisely in the revised manuscript. We do not argue that the police resources brought by \emph{Plan Cuadrante} play no role. They likely enable the successful implementation of the program, and for some outcomes the effects may indeed be larger in municipalities that received greater resource increases. In the absence of a natural experiment that would allow us to isolate the causal effect of resources from the organizational reform, we cannot fully disentangle the two channels in municipalities that received large resource increases. Our argument is different: the beat policing component of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}---the division of the municipal territory into quadrants with the permanent assignment of officers to those territories---plays a key role in explaining the results, over and above the resource channel. This is supported by the evidence in Figure \ref{fig:figure_resources}: while some treated municipalities received substantial increases in officers and patrol vehicles, others experienced changes very similar to those in the control group, and the program had large and statistically significant effects in this latter group across most outcomes. Reassuringly, municipalities receiving larger versus smaller resource increases---whether the split is based on the increase in police officers or in patrol vehicles---do not display differential pre-treatment trends in either the outcomes of interest or in police resources themselves, alleviating concerns that this split might itself be confounded by pre-existing differences across municipalities.\footnote{The p-values for the joint test of equal pre-trends between the two groups are, for the officers-based split and the patrol-vehicles-based split respectively: 0.823 and 0.048 for victimization, 0.059 and 0.379 for crime perception, 0.820 and 0.157 for investment in security measures, 0.213 and 0.419 for reported crime, 0.616 and 0.444 for police officers, and 0.405 and 0.157 for patrol vehicles.} We also note that our results are not in tension with the existing literature on police strength and crime. As \citet{chalfin2025investments} notes, the effectiveness of additional police resources depends crucially on how they are organized and deployed, suggesting that organizational reforms like \emph{Plan Cuadrante} can substantially improve public safety independently of changes in resource levels.

To provide further evidence, we split the sample of treated municipalities into those that experienced larger versus smaller resource increases with the adoption of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} and estimate the ATT separately for each subgroup. This subgroup analysis is in the spirit of the interaction suggested by the reviewer, although a direct interaction between the treatment dummy and a resource-increase indicator is not feasible within the Callaway and Sant'Anna framework.\footnote{Using OLS in this framework is not convenient due to the problem of negative weights \citep{GoodmanBacon}.} Additionally, we estimate the effect of \emph{Seguridad Ciudadana}, a program that increased police resources without any organizational component, and find no significant effects on public safety outcomes. Similarly, we do not find a significant association between police resources and most public safety outcomes in the post-\emph{Plan Cuadrante} period.

Following the reviewer's recommendation, we estimate in the table below the main regression of the effect of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} controlling for both the number of officers and the number of patrol vehicles, separately and jointly, to approximate a police production function as closely as possible. Three caveats are worth noting. First, police resources are affected by \emph{Plan Cuadrante} and are therefore potentially bad controls, but including them allows us to approximate the effect of the territorial reorganization component while holding resource levels constant. Second, the output of the estimation does not yield point estimates for the control variables. The only coefficient yield in each estimation is the ATT of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}. Third, information on resources is not available for the full sample, so adding these controls reduces the sample size. The results are reported in Table \ref{tab:results_controlbyresources} and show that, overall, the point estimates remain broadly stable when we add both patrol vehicles and police officers as controls. The statistical significance of some coefficients, however, is reduced. We believe this reflects a mechanical feature of the Callaway and Sant'Anna estimation procedure rather than weaker underlying effects. In this framework, control variables are not incorporated parametrically as in standard OLS but are instead used to construct the comparison group through outcome regression or propensity score weighting, introducing an additional estimation step that inflates mechanically the standard errors of the ATT estimates. Unlike in OLS, where adding controls typically reduces standard errors by absorbing residual variance, in the Callaway and Sant'Anna framework the uncertainty from estimating the outcome model or the propensity score propagates to the final estimates. The stability of the point estimates across specifications with and without controls for most outcomes supports this interpretation.

Taken together, while the results cannot rule out that police resources enable the successful implementation of the program, drive part of the effects of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} in some municipalities, they consistently highlight that the beat policing component of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}, namely the division of the territory into quadrants with the permanent assignment of officers to those territories, plays a crucial role in explaining the success of the program.

Finally, regarding the recommendation to use mediation analysis, the procedure proposed by \citet{imai2010identification} is unfortunately not directly applicable in the context of staggered difference-in-differences estimators, which limits our ability to formally quantify the share of the total effect attributable to the resource channel.
