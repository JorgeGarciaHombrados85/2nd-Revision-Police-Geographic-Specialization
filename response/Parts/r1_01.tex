%Plan Cuadrante appears to have changed several features of frontline policing simultaneously. Assigning officers to smaller quadrants changes not only the officer-area relationships, but also the spatial organization of police presence. Depending on quadrant size, number of officers, and deployment practices, the reform may have effectively changed police density and patrol intensity, as well as response times. Each of these margins could plausibly affect crime, victimization, perceptions of safety, and citizen cooperation. Greater local knowledge is one compelling mechanism within this bundle, but it is not separately observed from these other operational changes.

%I therefore think the paper should explicitly acknowledge that the results identify the effect of a broader organizational and deployment reform rather than the effect of local knowledge alone. A more defensible interpretation would be that Plan Cuadrante, combining permanent officer-to-quadrant allocation with an increase in police density, improved public safety and citizens' perceptions of the police. Even holding the total number of officers fixed, reorganizing officers across smaller territorial units may change the intensity and concentration of local police presence.

%One potentially useful exercise would be to characterize police density across quadrants, for example in terms of officers per square kilometer (or per capita). Thank you very much for this comment. We agree that, even in the absence of any increase in total resources, reassigning police officers within the same municipality and changes in policing strategies could increase police density in some areas, and that this could reduce crime if the additional density falls where it is most useful. This is a distinct channel from the accumulation of local knowledge that we have emphasized so far, and we agree that our interpretation should not attribute the results to local knowledge alone.

Unfortunately, the exercise you propose cannot be conducted with our data. Testing it would require information on police officers, polices resources and outcomes at the quadrant level, both before and after the creation of quadrants (i.e., before and after each municipality's adoption of \emph{Plan Cuadrante}). We requested this information from Carabineros on two separate occasions, but were only granted quadrant-level data on crime from 2013 onward, after the last ENUSC municipality in our sample had already adopted the program. For the rest of data, not even that. This precludes any before/after comparison at the quadrant level.

Two considerations are nonetheless worth noting. First, while the assignment of officers to quadrants was intended to follow a pre-established rule based on objective demand functions for police services, independent evaluations commissioned by Chile's Budget Office conclude that the spatial allocation of officers across quadrants within municipalities remained, in practice, at the discretion of the station commander (\emph{comisario}), both before and after \emph{Plan Cuadrante} \citep{DIPRES2014}---a point we already make in Section \ref{institutions}. Any reallocation of officers toward a more efficient spatial density would therefore likely itself be a product of the station commander's better knowledge of the territory, rather than a mechanically determined, independent channel. In other words, even if changes in police density are partly driving our results, this density is plausibly an outcome of the same territorial reorganization and strategy adjustment that we already discuss, rather than a separate mechanism running alongside it. Second, this is consistent with the heterogeneity evidence we already report in Section \ref{mechanisms}: effects on most outcomes are similar in magnitude between municipalities with control-like resource growth and those with much larger resource increases, which is hard to reconcile with a channel that operates mechanically through the level of police resources or density, and is more consistent with a broader reorganization of deployment and strategy.

In the revised paper, we would use these two points to soften the language attributing our effects to local knowledge specifically, replacing it with the broader, more defensible interpretation you suggest. Concretely, we would revise (i) the passage in the Introduction currently reading ``By remaining in the same territory, officers accumulated local knowledge of the area and its community and could adapt day-to-day practices---patrol routes and prevention activities---to local conditions,'' and (ii) the sentence in Section \ref{mechanisms} currently reading ``This pattern is more consistent with officers accumulating local knowledge and adapting prevention strategies to local conditions under stable territorial assignment than with a purely mechanical increase in patrol intensity,'' so that both instead attribute the effects to the reorganization of deployment---which may operate through local knowledge, through a more efficient spatial density of officers, or through some combination of the two---rather than to local knowledge alone. We have not made this change in the manuscript yet and will do so once we agree on the exact wording with you and the editor.
