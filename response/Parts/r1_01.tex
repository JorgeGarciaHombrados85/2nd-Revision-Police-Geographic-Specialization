We thank the reviewer for this comment and have substantially expanded Section 2. To address this point, we contacted Carabineros de Chile and held multiple meetings with its Research Department. These interactions gave us access to documents on the design and implementation of the program, as well as evaluation reports produced by the Chilean Budget Office (DIPRES).

Prior to \emph{Plan Cuadrante}, most police stations were responsible for the entire territory of their municipality, without any formal subdivision. Given that municipalities averaged more than 2,000 square kilometers and 100,000 inhabitants, this represented a large operational area. Officers were not permanently assigned to specific sub-areas within the municipality but instead rotated across the full territory in response to incoming calls and operational needs, coordinated centrally by the provincial communications center (CENCO). Although local commanders retained discretion over deployment decisions, this discretion was typically exercised reactively rather than used to systematically assign officers to fixed sub-areas within the municipality, as confirmed in our discussions with Carabineros' research department.

During the period studied, \emph{Plan Cuadrante} introduced two main changes. The first and most fundamental change was the permanent territorial assignment of officers to smaller, fixed catchment areas (quadrants). Rather than rotating across the full territory covered by the police station (typically the municipality), each officer or patrol unit was assigned to a specific quadrant as their primary area of responsibility, enabling the accumulation of local knowledge about the territory, its residents, and its crime patterns. This policing approach, known in the criminology literature as beat policing, is widely adopted around the world \citep{NASEM2018,PCSP2014}, yet its effects have not been rigorously assessed.

The second change was an increase in police resources in some municipalities. Some of the municipalities joining \emph{Plan Cuadrante} received additional officers and patrol vehicles with the goal of closing their pre-existing resource deficit, measured through a standardized methodology based on Equivalent Surveillance Units (UVEs) that quantified the gap between the demand for police services and the available supply \citep{DIPRES2007_largo, DIPRES2014}. This increase was financed primarily through a national expansion of Carabineros during the 2000s and 2010s, driven by successive legislative commitments that increased officer recruitment, benefiting both treated and control municipalities. The relocation of existing officers from control to treated municipalities was rare, as strict regulatory constraints on officer transfers made it very difficult to relocate officers before completing their assigned posting period.\footnote{See the Transfer Manual for Carabineros de Chile Personnel available at \url{https://www.carabineros.cl/transparencia/og/pdf/OG_2707_13112019.pdf}} The additional resources allocated specifically to \emph{Plan Cuadrante} municipalities were intended to close their deficit to the level required for the program's successful implementation, though budget constraints meant this goal was rarely fully achieved \citep{DIPRES2014}. Notably, while some treated municipalities received large resource increases, the bottom 40\% of the distribution of resource increases among treated municipalities received increases very similar to those observed in the control group.

Whether there was an increase in the proportion of resources allocated to front-line policing is something we cannot directly observe. However, this was not a distinguishing feature of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} and there was a single national set of operational guidelines applied to officers in both treated and control municipalities \citep{Manual_Carabineros2018,DIPRES2007_largo}. Any potential shift in the allocation of resources toward front-line policing would therefore reflect discretionary decisions by local station commanders, rather than an explicit tactical change embedded in the program.


The counterfactual is therefore a municipality of similar characteristics where officers continued to rotate across large catchment areas without permanent sub-area assignments and without the additional resources brought by \emph{Plan Cuadrante} to some municipalities. To disentangle the role of the permanent territorial assignment from that of additional resources, we leverage the substantial variation in the size of the resource increase across treated municipalities and show that the effects of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} are large and statistically significant even among municipalities that experienced no resource increases relative to control municipalities, suggesting that while resources might be key for the effective implementation of beat policing reforms or might explain part of the effects of \emph{Plan Cuadrante} in some municipalities, the permanent assignment to smaller catchment areas plays an important role beyond the simple resource channel.\footnote{We requested access to data on the demand and supply of UVEs to study the role of resources more directly, but this information was not shared. While our analysis does not yield a clean causal estimate of the resource component in isolation, the fact that the program had similarly large effects in municipalities whose resource increases were similar to those in the control group suggests that the beat policing component is key.} In line with this result, we show that increases in police resources that do not involve the beat policing component in Chile had limited effects on the same outcomes.
